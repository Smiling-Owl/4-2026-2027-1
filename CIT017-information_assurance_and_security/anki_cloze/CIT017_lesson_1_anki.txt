The {{c1::McCumber Cube}} is a {{c2::3D framework}} used to evaluate information system security by analyzing the interaction between three dimensions.
A {{c1::3D framework}} used to evaluate information system security by analyzing the interaction between three dimensions is known as the {{c2::McCumber Cube}}.
The three dimensions of the McCumber Cube are {{c1::Information States}}, {{c2::Security Goals (CIA Triad)}}, and {{c3::Countermeasures}}.
The three Information States (Data Status) in the McCumber Cube are {{c1::Transmission (Data in Motion)}} which refers to {{c2::data traveling over a network}}, {{c3::Storage (Data at Rest)}} which refers to {{c4::data sitting on a hard drive or database}}, and {{c5::Processing (Data in Use)}} which refers to {{c6::data currently being updated or calculated in RAM}}.
The Security Goals in the McCumber Cube are represented by the {{c1::CIA Triad}}, which stands for {{c2::Confidentiality}} (protecting against {{c3::unauthorized disclosure}}), {{c4::Integrity}} (protecting against {{c5::unauthorized modification}}), and {{c6::Availability}} (ensuring {{c7::timely and reliable access}}).
The three categories of Countermeasures in the McCumber Cube are {{c1::Technology}} which includes {{c2::hardware and software (e.g., Firewalls, Encryption)}}, {{c3::People}} which includes {{c4::awareness, training, and ethics}}, and {{c5::Policies & Practices}} which includes {{c6::Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), laws, and rules}}.
The three main functional controls in information security are {{c1::Prevention}} ({{c2::stopping the attack before it happens}}), {{c3::Detection}} ({{c4::identifying that an attack is happening or has already happened}}), and {{c5::Mitigation (Response)}} ({{c6::limiting the damage once an incident is detected}}).
The {{c1::AAA Framework}} stands for {{c2::Authentication}} (answering {{c3::"Who are you?"}} by {{c4::proving identity}}), {{c5::Authorization}} (answering {{c6::"What are you allowed to do?"}} by {{c7::defining permissions}}), and {{c8::Accounting / Auditing}} (answering {{c9::"What did you do?"}} by {{c10::tracking user actions}}).
The three authentication factors are {{c1::Knowledge (Password)}}, {{c2::Possession (OTP/Token)}}, and {{c3::Inherence (Biometrics)}}.
{{c1::Non-repudiation}} is a concept in Accounting that ensures {{c2::a user cannot deny performing an action because the logs provide definitive proof}}.
The principle of {{c1::Least Privilege}} states that users should be given {{c2::only the minimum permissions necessary to perform their job}}.
The principle of {{c1::Need-to-Know}} means that having clearance does not guarantee access; data is only accessible if {{c2::required for the current task}}.
The difference between Least Privilege and Need-to-Know is that {{c1::Least Privilege}} restricts permissions to the minimum necessary, whereas {{c2::Need-to-Know}} restricts access to data based on current task requirements despite clearance.
The principle of {{c1::Separation of Duties}} involves {{c2::splitting a sensitive task between two people to prevent fraud}}.
{{c1::Defense in Depth}} is the principle of {{c2::employing layered security}}.
The principle of {{c1::Fail Safe (Security by Default)}} dictates that if a system crashes, it must {{c2::fail into a "locked" state, not an "open" state}}.
{{c1::Security by Design}} means that security is {{c2::integrated into the software from the very first line of code, not added as an afterthought}}.
The {{c1::Principle of Complete Mediation}} states that {{c2::every access to every object must be checked for authority}}.
The principle of {{c1::Economy of Mechanism}} states that one should keep security design {{c2::simple and small}} to reduce the {{c3::"attack surface"}}.
The principle of {{c1::Open Design}} states that security should not rely on {{c2::the secrecy of the design (Security through Obscurity)}}.
