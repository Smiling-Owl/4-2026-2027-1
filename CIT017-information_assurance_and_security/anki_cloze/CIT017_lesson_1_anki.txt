The {{c1::McCumber Cube}} is a {{c2::3D framework}} used to evaluate information system security by analyzing the interaction between three dimensions.
A {{c1::3D framework}} used to evaluate information system security by analyzing the interaction between three dimensions is known as the {{c2::McCumber Cube}}.
The three dimensions of the McCumber Cube are {{c1::Information States}}, {{c2::Security Goals (CIA Triad)}}, and {{c3::Countermeasures}}.
The three Information States (Data Status) in the McCumber Cube are {{c1::Transmission (Data in Motion)}}, {{c2::Storage (Data at Rest)}}, and {{c3::Processing (Data in Use)}}.
In the McCumber Cube, {{c1::Transmission}} is also known as {{c2::Data in Motion}} and refers to {{c3::data traveling over a network}}.
In the McCumber Cube, {{c1::Storage}} is also known as {{c2::Data at Rest}} and refers to {{c3::data sitting on a hard drive or database}}.
In the McCumber Cube, {{c1::Processing}} is also known as {{c2::Data in Use}} and refers to {{c3::data currently being updated or calculated in RAM}}.
The Security Goals in the McCumber Cube are represented by the {{c1::CIA Triad}}, which stands for {{c2::Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability}}.
{{c1::Confidentiality}} is the security goal of protecting against {{c2::unauthorized disclosure}}.
{{c1::Integrity}} is the security goal of protecting against {{c2::unauthorized modification}}.
{{c1::Availability}} is the security goal of ensuring {{c2::timely and reliable access}}.
The three categories of Countermeasures in the McCumber Cube are {{c1::Technology}}, {{c2::People}}, and {{c3::Policies & Practices}}.
In the McCumber Cube, the Technology countermeasure includes {{c1::hardware and software (e.g., Firewalls, Encryption)}}.
In the McCumber Cube, the People countermeasure includes {{c1::awareness, training, and ethics}}.
In the McCumber Cube, the Policies & Practices countermeasure includes {{c1::Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), laws, and rules}}.
The three main functional controls in information security are {{c1::Prevention}}, {{c2::Detection}}, and {{c3::Mitigation (Response)}}.
{{c1::Prevention}} is the functional control of {{c2::stopping the attack before it happens}}.
{{c1::Detection}} is the functional control of {{c2::identifying that an attack is happening or has already happened}}.
{{c1::Mitigation (Response)}} is the functional control of {{c2::limiting the damage once an incident is detected}}.
The {{c1::AAA Framework}} stands for {{c2::Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting / Auditing}}.
{{c1::Authentication}} answers the question {{c2::"Who are you?"}} by {{c3::proving identity}}.
The three authentication factors are {{c1::Knowledge (Password)}}, {{c2::Possession (OTP/Token)}}, and {{c3::Inherence (Biometrics)}}.
{{c1::Authorization}} answers the question {{c2::"What are you allowed to do?"}} by {{c3::defining permissions}}.
{{c1::Accounting / Auditing}} answers the question {{c2::"What did you do?"}} by {{c3::tracking user actions}}.
{{c1::Non-repudiation}} is a concept in Accounting that ensures {{c2::a user cannot deny performing an action because the logs provide definitive proof}}.
The principle of {{c1::Least Privilege}} states that users should be given {{c2::only the minimum permissions necessary to perform their job}}.
The principle of {{c1::Need-to-Know}} means that having clearance does not guarantee access; data is only accessible if {{c2::required for the current task}}.
The difference between Least Privilege and Need-to-Know is that {{c1::Least Privilege}} restricts permissions to the minimum necessary, whereas {{c2::Need-to-Know}} restricts access to data based on current task requirements despite clearance.
The principle of {{c1::Separation of Duties}} involves {{c2::splitting a sensitive task between two people to prevent fraud}}.
{{c1::Defense in Depth}} is the principle of {{c2::employing layered security}}.
The principle of {{c1::Fail Safe (Security by Default)}} dictates that if a system crashes, it must {{c2::fail into a "locked" state, not an "open" state}}.
{{c1::Security by Design}} means that security is {{c2::integrated into the software from the very first line of code, not added as an afterthought}}.
The {{c1::Principle of Complete Mediation}} states that {{c2::every access to every object must be checked for authority}}.
The principle of {{c1::Economy of Mechanism}} states that one should keep security design {{c2::simple and small}} to reduce the {{c3::"attack surface"}}.
The principle of {{c1::Open Design}} states that security should not rely on {{c2::the secrecy of the design (Security through Obscurity)}}.
